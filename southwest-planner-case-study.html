Designing Vacation Allocation for Southwest Planners

Replacing a multi-tab macro spreadsheet with a compliance-aware planning tool that lets schedulers model scenarios, catch contract violations in real time, and adapt when the contract changes.

Company Southwest Airlines (via Amdocs Studios)
Timeline 2022–Present
Role Principal Designer
Focus Enterprise Tools, Compliance Design, Data-Dense Interfaces, Design Systems

The Problem

Before vacation bidding opens for 11,000 pilots, planners decide how many vacation slots are available each week — by base, by role, by month — while staying within SWAPA contract requirements. Get it wrong and pilots bid against inaccurate data. Miss a contract minimum and the airline risks a grievance.

They were doing this in a multi-tab Excel spreadsheet with macros. This wasn't just a UX problem. It was a systemic risk hiding in plain sight.

The tool planners used before — a multi-tab spreadsheet with macros and a manual CSV export to feed the bidding system. One wrong formula, one missed update, and compliance breaks for the entire bid cycle.

Before I Touched Figma

Rather than accepting the problem as defined, I mapped the real constraints — some inherited, some negotiable.

Expert users, not typical users

Planners know the SWAPA contract in detail. The tool had to match their mental model, not simplify it away.

Layered compliance requirements

Weekly, monthly, and seasonal minimums — separately for Captains and First Officers, across every base. Any tool that couldn't surface all of it simultaneously would replicate the spreadsheet's core failure.

Contract change as a design requirement

I identified early that designing around current contract values would create engineering debt every time the contract was renegotiated. Making minimums editable by planners was a strategic decision I brought to the team, not a feature request.

Jetstream design system

No custom components. The challenge was achieving the data density this tool required using existing patterns — and identifying where the system needed to grow for operational use cases it wasn't built for.

Discovery

With a small team of domain experts as users, I made a deliberate call to run collaborative working sessions rather than traditional usability research. Planners needed to map their own workflow to surface where the real friction lived.

User story mapping session with the planning team — surfacing the scenario modeling planners were already doing mentally, but had no tool support for.

What emerged reframed the entire project. Planners weren't just entering numbers — they were running scenarios in their heads. What happens if we boost December slots at DEN? Does that break a seasonal minimum somewhere else? The spreadsheet forced them to recalculate manually every time they changed an assumption.

The real problem wasn't data entry. It was decision support.

The Solution

Three capabilities working as a system.

Scenario modeling. Four named strategies, compliance status at a glance, and clear indication of which scenario is active. Summer-Heavy shows Errors — a violation that must be resolved before it can go live.

Real-time compliance feedback. Week 3 for First Officers is flagged inline: "Weekly minimum must be ≥ 1.5%." The right panel shows accrual weeks and seasonal minimums simultaneously — required versus actual, with deltas — so planners see the full compliance picture without switching views.

Seasonal minimums at a glance. Seasonal Monthly Minimums expanded: period, minimum, required, actual, difference. Planners see at a glance where they stand across the full year — and where they have room to make strategic adjustments.

Editable contract minimums. Contractual Minimums tab. Weekly, monthly, and seasonal minimums are independently editable — a deliberate architectural decision to ensure the tool's longevity across contract negotiations.

Scenario modeling

Planners create named scenarios — Baseline, Holiday Buffer, Summer-Heavy, DEN Winter Boost — each representing a different allocation strategy. Scenarios are independently editable, compliance-checked, and comparable. The active scenario feeds the bidding system.

Real-time compliance feedback

Every allocation decision is validated against the SWAPA contract as planners work. Inline error messages explain exactly what's failing and what the requirement is — planners don't hunt for problems, the tool surfaces them in context.

Editable contract minimums

When the SWAPA contract changes, planners update the tool themselves. No engineering involvement. Default and seasonal minimums are editable directly from Settings — keeping the tool accurate across contract cycles and giving the operational team ownership over their own system.

System Impact

The planner tool and the pilot-facing vacation bidding redesign are two sides of the same system. The confidence indicators pilots see on their bid groups are only meaningful if the underlying allocation data is accurate and compliant. Designing both ends gave me visibility into how planner decisions ripple through to the pilot experience — and shaped design choices on both sides.

What This Work Unlocked

Collaborative discovery scales to expert users

Facilitated working sessions produced better insights faster than traditional research would have — and built the cross-functional alignment that made the project move. This approach is now part of how the broader design team handles stakeholder-heavy projects.

Compliance feedback changes behavior

Planners shifted from building complete scenarios and then checking compliance, to catching and correcting issues in real time. The tool changed how they work, not just what they use.

Designing for change reduces organizational debt

Editable minimums eliminated a recurring category of engineering requests. Giving operational teams ownership over their own configuration made the tool sustainable across contract cycles.
