Designing Airline Operations at Scale

Building sustainable design processes while supporting 5 products, 10 apps, and 3 product owners as a solo designer.

Company Southwest Airlines (via Amdocs Studios)
Timeline 2022-Present
Role Principal Designer
Focus Enterprise Tools, Process Design, Research

The Challenge

I'm the solo designer supporting Southwest's crew operations—5 products, 10 apps, 3 product owners, and 3 dev teams. My users range from flight attendants and pilots to planners and schedulers who manage airline operations.

But I'm not actually alone: I also support 3 other designers. I'm responsible for approving their work and facilitating weekly design reviews. This adds another layer of complexity—I'm designing products while also ensuring quality and consistency across a growing team.

The scale is one challenge. The complexity is another. Airline crew bidding involves seniority rules, union contract terms (SWAPA), federal regulations, and preferences that affect people's lives—their vacation time, their holidays with family, their income.

Currently, pilots can use an antiquated internal system or pay for a third-party app. Neither is great. We're building something better, but designing for highly regulated, safety-critical operations means I can't just ship fast and iterate. Every decision needs careful validation.

The real challenge: How do you design at this scale, with this complexity, while also growing and guiding a design team, without becoming a bottleneck?

Supporting 5 products and 10 apps across Flight Attendants, Pilots, Planners, and Schedulers—leading a team of 4 designers while working with 3 product owners and 3 development teams.

Building a Sustainable Process

When I started on Southwest, there was no clear intake system. Requests came from three different product owners at unpredictable times. I was constantly context-switching, priorities weren't clear, and stakeholders were frustrated.

I needed to design the system that would let me design the systems.

The intake process

Working with a product manager, I experimented with different approaches and landed on a Jira form that puts design requests into my backlog. Product owners submit stories, and the product lead decides priorities during PI planning.

The review workflow

For every UX story: design → design team review → product/tech review → business/user approval. This ensures quality and buy-in, but more importantly, it scales. Anyone can follow this process.

Weekly design reviews

I facilitate weekly design reviews where our team of 4 designers shares work, gives feedback, and maintains consistency across products. I'm responsible for approving work before it moves forward, ensuring quality and alignment with design standards.

Figma annotations

I've been experimenting with Figma annotations to communicate implementation details while tracking approvals. Each annotation documents design decisions, specs, and sign-offs—all in one place.

Figma annotations serve double duty—communicating implementation specs to developers while tracking stakeholder approvals. This system reduces miscommunication and creates accountability across teams.

Example: Flight Ops Vacation Bid

My current project is redesigning how pilots bid for vacation. The existing system is so antiquated that many pilots pay for third-party apps rather than use the free internal tool.

The complexity: Pilots bid by seniority, and bid rounds are dictated by the SWAPA union contract. Miss your bid window or misunderstand the rules? You might lose out on Christmas with your family.

The technical constraint: The app is responsive web designed for landscape mode on pilots' iPads—a specific use case that shapes every design decision.

The RITE study

I partnered with a researcher who facilitated sessions with pilots to test different approaches to the bidding flow. While the researcher ran the sessions, I synthesized the findings and created the readout presentation for stakeholders.

Four approaches to vacation bidding, each tested with pilots in RITE sessions. No single concept emerged as the clear winner—each had unique strengths that informed the final design direction.

Key Findings

No single concept was a clear winner—each had strengths. Most pilots didn't understand key contract terms like "bid group."

My approach going forward: Synthesize the best elements from each concept and run another round of testing. This iterative, evidence-based approach is critical when you're designing for people's livelihoods.

Key findings from the RITE study presented to stakeholders. Understanding that pilots didn't grasp "bid group" terminology led to significant design changes in how we explain union contract terms.

Working at Scale

Jetstream Design System

I work within Southwest's Jetstream design system, which provides constraints but also consistency. Rather than designing from scratch every time, I leverage existing patterns and components, adapting them for crew operations use cases.

Managing priorities

With 3 product owners and 10 apps, I can't work on everything. The PI planning process helps, but I also communicate clearly about capacity, timelines, and tradeoffs. I've learned to say "yes, and here's when" instead of just "yes."

Cross-team collaboration

I work with 3 dev teams at different speeds and in different time zones. Weekly syncs, clear documentation, and early tech lead involvement keep us aligned.

Impact

More importantly, I've built a design process that works at scale. The intake system, review workflow, and collaboration patterns I've established will continue working long after I move on.

This work is ongoing. The vacation bidding tool is still in development, the processes I've built continue to evolve, and I'm learning every day how to design better at scale.

What I Learned

You can't design at scale alone—you have to design the system.

Building intake processes, review workflows, and clear prioritization isn't glamorous, but it's what makes good design possible when you're supporting this many products.

Leading designers is different from doing design

Facilitating weekly design reviews and approving work taught me that good leadership means asking the right questions, not giving all the answers. My job is to help designers think through problems, not solve them for them.

Research saves time, even when it's slow. The RITE study revealed misunderstandings that would have led to a failed product. Iterating early with users is always faster than fixing a broken tool in production.

Constraints are features. Working within the Jetstream design system and SWAPA contract rules felt limiting at first, but constraints force creative problem-solving. I can't just design anything—I have to design the right thing.

Communication is design work. Managing 3 POs, 3 dev teams, multiple stakeholders, and 3 designers requires as much craft as the interfaces themselves. Clear communication, expectation-setting, and transparency are how you scale.

Experiment with your own processes. The Jira intake form, Figma annotations, review workflows, design reviews—none of these existed when I started. I built them by experimenting, getting feedback, and iterating. Design operations is design work too.

That's the work I love—not just making products, but making the systems that make great products possible and helping designers grow.
